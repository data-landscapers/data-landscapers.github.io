# I'm looking for any holdings, directly or indirectly, that Google/Alphabet owns in Africa

Alphabet/Google has a mix of direct equity stakes and broader infrastructure or fund exposures in Africa, but there is no single comprehensive public register, so what follows is a non‑exhaustive map of what can be documented from open sources.[^1][^2][^3][^4][^5]

### 1. Direct equity stakes in African companies

Known, disclosed equity investments include:[^2][^3][^4][^5][^1]

- **Cassava Technologies (Pan‑African)** – Google took a direct minority equity stake as part of a 90 million USD round in 2024 to support data infrastructure (Liquid Intelligent Technologies, Equiano/Umoja terrestrial links, cloud and AI services).[^4][^1]
- **SafeBoda (Uganda, Nigeria)** – first disclosed investment from Google’s Africa Investment Fund in 2021; size undisclosed but confirmed equity.[^3][^5]
- **Moniepoint (Nigeria)** – Series C round (about 110 million USD) led or joined by Google’s Africa Investment Fund; implies a material minority stake.[^2]
- **Leta (Kenya)** – seed round participation by Google’s Africa Investment Fund.[^2]

These are direct or fund‑linked equity positions; there are likely additional undisclosed or very small cheques that do not appear in high‑level press.

### 2. Google’s Africa Investment Fund portfolio

Google created a dedicated Africa Investment Fund (50 million USD+ envelope, within a broader 1 billion USD Africa pledge) for early‑ and growth‑stage startups:[^5][^3][^2]

- Mandate: minority equity in tech‑led African startups, with an emphasis on fintech, logistics, SaaS and digital platforms.[^3][^5][^2]
- Publicly confirmed portfolio so far includes SafeBoda (Uganda), Moniepoint (Nigeria), Leta (Kenya), and potentially other undisclosed deals in Nigeria, Kenya, Egypt and South Africa.[^5][^3][^2]

In legal terms, these holdings sit in Alphabet/Google investment vehicles but are ultimately controlled by Alphabet Inc. as parent.

### 3. Infrastructure and data‑center related holdings

On the infrastructure side, Google is mainly the owner/operator or anchor investor rather than holding listed equity in local telecoms, but some structures do create asset‑level “holdings” in Africa:[^6][^7][^8][^9][^10][^1][^4]

- **Equiano subsea cable** – Google financed and owns this subsea cable system connecting Portugal to several West and Southern African countries, with landing partnerships in Nigeria, Namibia, South Africa and others; local partners may co‑invest in landing stations, but Google retains primary economic control over the cable asset.[^7][^8][^6]
- **Umoja subsea and terrestrial route** – Google is investing in the new fiber route linking Africa to Australia, with a terrestrial leg built in partnership with Cassava’s Liquid Intelligent Technologies across multiple African states; ownership is structured via Google and network partners, giving Google significant rights over capacity and infrastructure.[^7][^1][^4]
- **Four African “infrastructure hubs” (landing stations + data centres)** – Google is building four fully funded infrastructure hubs in Africa (north, south, east, west) to connect Equiano and Umoja; these are owned and financed directly by Google, though land, power and last‑mile elements often involve local partners.[^11][^8][^9][^10]
- **Google‑operated data centres in Africa** – Google has at least one operational cloud data centre in Africa and is expanding regional infrastructure; these facilities are wholly owned or controlled by Google entities even where they sit inside co‑location or JV arrangements.[^12][^1][^4]

These are not shareholdings in African corporations in the classical sense, but they are capital assets located in Africa and fully owned or majority‑controlled by Alphabet subsidiaries.

### 4. Indirect and programmatic exposure (non‑equity)

Alphabet/Google also deploys capital and influence through grants, accelerators, and research initiatives that do not necessarily yield equity:[^13][^14][^10][^6][^11]

- **Google for Startups Accelerator Africa / Black Founders Fund Africa** – non‑dilutive funding and credits to >150 African startups; these programs generally do not grant Google an ownership stake but can create strong strategic ties.[^13][^11]
- **AI and research hubs** – Google has funded AI research centres and community hubs (e.g., Accra, Ghana) and committed tens of millions of dollars to AI ecosystem grants across Africa; again, these are not equity stakes but do embed Google in national research ecosystems.[^14][^6][^12]
- **Skills, connectivity and product programs** – large‑scale training programs, cloud credits, and government partnerships around digital transformation and AI, which shape dependency but do not show up as “holdings.”[^6][^12][^11]


### 5. Gaps and limits in public information

- Alphabet does not publish a consolidated, Africa‑specific holdings list; outside of high‑profile deals like Cassava or SafeBoda, smaller or earlier‑stage investments may be undisclosed or only visible in local registries or niche VC databases.[^1][^4][^3][^5]
- Many of Google’s African relationships are contractual (capacity, cloud, advertising, public‑sector digitisation) rather than equity, so traditional “ownership” mapping misses a large share of its real power over infrastructure and services.[^12][^11][^6][^7]
