# Comprehensive Literature Review: Google's Activities in Africa (2021–2026)

## Introduction

Over the past five years, Google and its parent company Alphabet have dramatically expanded their footprint across Africa, investing more than $1 billion in infrastructure, AI research, digital skills training, and startup support. This literature review surveys the available books, academic articles, NGO reports, policy documents, and substantial journalism covering these activities. It is organized thematically to capture both the scope of Google's engagement and the critical responses it has provoked.[^1][^2]

***

## 1. Infrastructure and Connectivity

### 1.1 The $1 Billion Investment Pledge

In October 2021, Google CEO Sundar Pichai announced a $1 billion commitment to Africa over five years, targeting affordable internet access, digital transformation for businesses, and tech startup support. By mid-2024, Google had invested more than $900 million toward this pledge, and by September 2025, the company announced it had exceeded the commitment early.[^2][^3][^4][^1]

**Key sources:**
- **Public First, *Google's Impact in Sub-Saharan Africa* (2024)** — An independent report commissioned by Google evaluating the economic and social impact of Google's products and investments. It estimates that Google Search, Play, YouTube, Cloud, and advertising tools delivered $16 billion of additional economic activity for Sub-Saharan businesses, nonprofits, publishers, creators, and developers in 2023. It further estimates that every $1 invested in digital technology will return over $2 for the region's wider economy by 2030.[^3]

### 1.2 The Equiano Subsea Cable

Google's private Equiano subsea cable, running from Portugal along Africa's entire western coast to South Africa, is among the most studied of Google's Africa investments. Named after Nigerian-born writer and abolitionist Olaudah Equiano, the cable uses space-division multiplexing (SDM) technology and offers approximately 20 times more network capacity than the previous cable serving the region.[^5]

**Key sources:**
- **Africa Practice and Genesis Analytics, *Equiano Economic Impact Assessments* (2021–2022)** — Google commissioned country-level economic impact assessments for Nigeria, South Africa, Namibia, and Togo. For Nigeria, Equiano was estimated to create 1.6 million indirect jobs and add $10.1 billion to GDP by 2025, with internet retail prices declining 21% and speeds growing sixfold. For Togo, the cable was projected to add $351 million in economic output and create approximately 37,000 jobs. These reports constitute some of the most detailed quantitative analyses of a single infrastructure investment's projected impact on African economies.[^6][^7][^8][^9]
- **Omdia (2024), *Google's Equiano subsea cable lands in Africa***: An industry analysis noting that Equiano would reduce internet access prices by 21% and provide 20 times more capacity than previous cables, while situating Google's investment in the broader context of hyperscaler competition in Africa.[^10]

### 1.3 Umoja Cable and Africa Connect

In 2024, Google announced the Umoja cable — the first fiber optic route to directly connect Africa with Australia, running through Kenya, Uganda, Rwanda, Democratic Republic of Congo, Zambia, Zimbabwe, and South Africa. Together with Equiano and Google's Cloud region in Johannesburg (operational since January 2024), these assets form Google's **Africa Connect** infrastructure program. In September 2025, Google announced four new strategic subsea cable connectivity hubs in the north, south, east, and west of Africa, aiming to create new digital corridors within and beyond the continent.[^11][^12][^13][^1]

### 1.4 Project Taara and Project Loon

**Alphabet's X Lab** has also pursued connectivity projects in Africa:
- **Project Loon**, which used stratospheric balloons to provide internet, partnered with Telkom Kenya in 2018 before being shut down in January 2021 due to high costs. It is frequently cited in literature about experimental connectivity initiatives.[^14]
- **Project Taara** uses free-space optical communications (lasers) to beam internet data without fiber cables. It has been deployed across 13 countries, with Liquid Intelligent Technologies rolling it out across more than 50 communities in seven African countries. A notable achievement was beaming 700TB of data across the Congo River to connect Brazzaville and Kinshasa.[^15][^16][^17]

### 1.5 CSquared

Google launched **CSquared** in 2013 to build metropolitan fiber-optic networks in African cities. By 2017, it had become a four-way partnership with Google, Convergence Partners, IFC, and Mitsui & Co., with a consolidated fund of $100 million. CSquared has built over 3,000 km of fiber across Ghana, Uganda, Liberia, Togo, and the DRC, and in 2025 announced a major West African backbone expansion connecting coastal and landlocked ECOWAS countries. CSquared's open-access, carrier-neutral model is also featured in the Equiano EIAs as key to distributing cable capacity to downstream ISPs.[^18][^19][^20][^21][^10]

### 1.6 Google Cloud in Johannesburg

Google Cloud's first African cloud region was launched in Johannesburg in early 2024. South Africa's President Cyril Ramaphosa characterized it as "a valuable investment" fostering innovation across the economy. This is a critical piece for enterprises seeking to run AI and data-intensive workloads locally on the continent.[^13]

## 2. AI Research and Applications

### 2.1 Google Research Africa (Accra and Nairobi)

Google has established AI research centers in **Accra, Ghana** and **Nairobi, Kenya**, developing solutions focused on healthcare, food security, mapping, languages, and climate. These labs are referenced in a growing body of academic literature examining Africa's emerging role in the global AI ecosystem.[^22][^23]

**Key sources:**
- **IJPREMS (2025), *Africa's Emerging Role in the Global AI Ecosystem*** — An academic paper citing Google's AI lab in Ghana as dedicated to creating AI solutions for African languages and enhancing information access for marginalized groups.[^23]
- **CIPIT, *The State of AI in Africa Report*** — A landmark assessment of AI development across the continent, noting the roles of both external actors like Google and homegrown initiatives.[^24]

### 2.2 WAXAL: African Language AI

Google Research Africa's **WAXAL** initiative is building foundational speech-to-speech AI infrastructure for low-resource African languages. Rather than focusing solely on text translation, the project creates large-scale, high-quality speech datasets. Universities such as Makerere University (Uganda) and the University of Ghana led data collection, retaining ownership of the datasets released as open source. In Ghana, the **UGSpeechData initiative** produced over 5,000 hours of audio data, enabling development of a maternal health chatbot in local languages and work on atypical speech recognition.[^25]

**Key competitors and comparators** documented in this literature include Masakhane (grassroots NLP research collective covering 45+ African languages), Lelapa AI (South Africa, focused on isiZulu, Sesotho, and Afrikaans NLP), and Lesan AI (Ethiopia, focused on Amharic, Tigrinya, and Oromo).[^25]

### 2.3 Open Buildings Dataset

Google Research Africa launched the **Open Buildings** dataset in 2021, using AI and satellite imagery to map building footprints across the Global South. Now in its third version, it contains polygons for 1.8 billion buildings. In Uganda, nonprofit Sunbird AI used the dataset to work with the government to identify areas suited for microgrids or solar panels.[^26]

**Key critical source:**
- **Global Witness, *Mapping buildings across Africa with AI*** — Acknowledged the dataset's utility for tracking deforestation and population estimation, but raised questions about surveillance, global inequity (the dataset exists *because* African countries lack equivalent national mapping resources), and the colonial dynamics of mapping Africa from a nominally neutral but Northern-anchored perspective.[^27]

### 2.4 Flood Forecasting AI

Google's AI-powered flood forecasting system, published in **Nature** (2024), demonstrated that machine learning can improve flood forecasting in data-scarce regions of Africa and Asia to levels comparable to European forecasts. Google's **Flood Hub** launched in 2022 with 15 African countries and expanded to 80 countries by 2023, covering 460 million people globally. In Mozambique, Google partnered with GiveDirectly to use forecasts to distribute $200 per person in advance of flooding.[^28][^29][^30][^31]

**Key academic source:**
- **Nevo et al. (2022), *Flood forecasting with machine learning models in an operational framework*, HESS** — A highly cited paper (263 citations) describing Google's operational flood forecasting system with a focus on ungauged basins.[^32]

### 2.5 Google DeepMind in Africa

Google DeepMind has engaged with Africa primarily through **AlphaFold**, its protein structure prediction tool. The open-access database of over 200 million protein structures has accelerated research on antimicrobial resistance and malaria. DeepMind's **Impact Accelerator** supports talent development, including a Master's program in AI for Science at the African Institute for Mathematical Sciences and BioStruct-Africa capacity-building workshops. DeepMind has also developed models like **Gemma** that operate on standard laptops, enabling AI research in low-resource environments.[^33]

In September 2025, **ACE-Uganda and Google DeepMind** announced a partnership to advance bioinformatics research, providing workshops on AlphaFold and AlphaMissense integration, mentorship, and compute resources for scientists in Uganda, Kenya, Rwanda, and Tanzania. Google also sponsors the **Deep Learning Indaba**, Africa's premier ML and AI community gathering.[^34][^35]

### 2.6 Health AI

In 2025, Google co-hosted an **Africa-wide Data Science for Health Ideathon** in partnership with SisonkeBiotik, Ro'ya, and DS-I Africa at the Deep Learning Indaba in Kigali, Rwanda. Teams used Google's open health AI models (MedGemma, TxGemma, MedSigLIP) to tackle challenges from cervical cancer screening to maternal health.[^36]

**Key academic source:**
- **"Nteasee: Understanding Needs in AI for Health in Africa" (ACM, 2025)** — A qualitative study of AI's potential for health in Africa from an algorithmic fairness perspective.[^37]

## 3. Startup and Entrepreneurship Support

### 3.1 Google for Startups Black Founders Fund

Established in 2020/2021, the **Black Founders Fund (BFF)** for Africa provided equity-free cash awards (up to $150,000 each), Google Cloud credits, ads support, and mentorship to Black-led startups across Africa. The cumulative program awarded more than $40 million globally and supported over 220 African startups.[^38][^39][^40]

**Key sources:**
- **Google for Startups, *Black Founders Fund Impact Report* (2024)** — Reports that BFF-backed companies collectively raised $379 million in follow-on funding and created over 6,000 jobs. Revenue growth among BFF-supported companies outpaced industry peers by 61% (2020–2022). Women hold over 50% of leadership roles in these startups, with 68% being women-led or co-founded. The report estimates a hypothetical 5.8x return on capital deployed and identifies a nearly $10 billion investment opportunity gap in Africa for Black entrepreneurs.[^41][^40]

Kenya stands out with 26 BFF-backed startups collectively raising nearly $100 million in follow-on funding and creating over 1,000 jobs.[^41]

### 3.2 Africa to Silicon Valley (A2SV)

Google's partnership with **Africa to Silicon Valley (A2SV)** has trained over 250 students since 2019, achieving a 100% job placement rate at major tech companies including Google, Amazon, and Microsoft.[^3]

## 4. Digital Skills and Education

### 4.1 Digital Skills Training at Scale

Google reports training **7 million Africans** in digital skills and has committed to training an additional 3 million students, young people, and teachers by 2030. In 2023 alone, Google's Africa-focused training programs taught 6.5 million people. Google's **Hustle Academy** provides small businesses with a free, virtual five-day bootcamp.[^42][^43][^44][^45]

### 4.2 African Union Partnerships

Google has established multiple formal partnerships with the African Union:
- **ECA-Google MoU (2024)** covering digital skills, startup development, financial inclusion, cybersecurity, and AI policy research.[^46]
- **AUC-Google MoU (February 2026)** — A landmark partnership to advance AI and digital transformation, guided by the AU's Continental AI Strategy and Digital Transformation Strategy for Africa (2020–2030). This included announcement of an AI readiness training program for African public officials and free access to Gemini Pro and NotebookLM in local languages including Amharic.[^47]
- **ECA-Google Regional Data Commons for Africa (2025)** — Backed by a Google.org $750,000 contribution, this initiative aims to establish Africa's interoperable and AI-enabled public data infrastructure.[^45]

### 4.3 University AI Tools Access

In 2025, Google began offering free **Gemini AI Pro Plan** access to eligible university students (ages 18+) in Ghana, Kenya, Nigeria, Rwanda, South Africa, and Zimbabwe for one year.[^48]

### 4.4 AI/Robotics Training with ITU

In September 2025, Google partnered with the **ITU** and **will.i.am** to bring AI and robotics training to students across Africa, with a $1 million commitment announced at the UN General Assembly.[^49]

### 4.5 University Research Funding

Google has provided African universities and research institutions with over **$17 million** in funding, curriculum, training, compute, and access to advanced AI models over the past four years, with an additional **$9 million** planned.[^42]

## 6. Critical Perspectives

### 6.1 Digital Colonialism

A substantial and growing body of academic literature frames Big Tech's activities in Africa — including Google's — through a **digital colonialism** lens.

**Key sources:**
- **Coleman, D. (2019), "Digital Colonialism: The 21st Century Scramble for Africa through the Extraction and Control of User Data," *Michigan Journal of Race & Law*** — A foundational and widely cited (272+ citations) paper arguing that large-scale tech companies extract, analyze, and own African user data for profit with nominal benefit to data sources. It examines the limits of data protection laws (including Kenya's 2018 Data Protection Bill) as purported solutions.[^52]
- **Obi, P.A. (2024), "Re-Imagining Digital Colonialism in Africa, Silicon Valley Big Techs, and the Politics of Prosumer Capitalism in Nigeria," *tripleC*** — Uses Nigeria as a prism to examine how platforms like Google, Facebook, and Twitter appropriate economic power through digital colonialism and prosumer capitalism.[^53]
- **Singler & Babalola (2024), "Digital Colonialism Beyond Surveillance Capitalism: Coloniality of Knowledge," *Social & Legal Studies*** — Critically analyzes how digitalization in Global South contexts is driven by profit motives of transnational corporations engaged in economic extraction and control of technological infrastructures.[^54]
- **Cambridge University Press (2025), "Digital Colonialism and the Role of Local Intermediaries," *Business and Human Rights Journal*** — Examines how Big Tech and local intermediaries perpetuate data exploitation. Argues that Africa risks becoming "data-rich but power-poor" and proposes Afrocentric data governance.[^55]
- **JSTOR (2021), "Big Tech's Influx into Africa: A Case of Power Without Responsibility"** — Characterizes a contemporary "Scramble for Data".[^56]

### 6.2 Taxation and Revenue

**Key sources:**
- **TaxWatch (2021), *Tech giants undermine African countries by failing to collect VAT*** — Found that Microsoft, Google, and Facebook were not collecting VAT on sales in most African countries, even where they had local offices. In South Africa, where specific digital services VAT legislation was introduced in 2014, an additional R600 million per year was collected.[^57]
- **South Centre (2023), *Taxation of Digital Services: What Hope for African States?*** — Examines how the international tax framework targets approximately 100 multinationals, especially digital giants (Google, Apple, Facebook, Amazon, Microsoft), and analyzes Africa's disadvantaged position.[^58]
- **African Leadership Magazine (2025), *Africa's Digital Tax Regime and the Battle for Access*** — Documents Kenya's 1.5% digital services tax (2021), Nigeria's 6% tax on non-resident digital companies, and Uganda's controversial social media tax (2018). Argues that fragmented tax regimes create compliance nightmares and risk undermining the AfCFTA's single digital market vision.[^59]
- **Banga (2025), "Trading away tax sovereignty? How trade rules shape taxation," *Journal of International Economic Law*** — Develops a framework examining how trade rules constrain African countries' ability to tax digital services.[^60]

### 6.3 Content Moderation Failures

**Key sources:**
- **Global Witness and Legal Resources Centre (2023)** — Investigation showing YouTube (alongside Facebook, TikTok, and X/Twitter) approved adverts containing extreme misogynistic hate speech in South Africa in English, Afrikaans, Xhosa, and Zulu. YouTube approved all 40 test ads (flagging 21 with "limited" status). Google did not respond to requests for comment. The report notes that Google "cut a unit responsible for misinformation, radicalization, and toxicity by a third".[^61]
- **Tech Policy Press (2025), *Why Africa Is Sounding the Alarm on Platforms' Shift in Content Moderation*** — Documents the African Commission on Human and Peoples' Rights' Resolution 630/2025, expressing concern over regressions in content moderation by platforms including YouTube, which adopted Community Notes systems while cutting professional fact-checking.[^62]

### 6.4 Outsourced Labor

While much of the outsourced content moderation literature focuses on Meta/Facebook and Sama in Kenya, Google is implicated in the broader ecosystem:
- **TIME Magazine (2023)** reported that Sama, a San Francisco-based firm employing workers in Kenya, Uganda, and India, labels data for "Silicon Valley clients like Google, Meta and Microsoft".[^63]
- **African Content Moderators Union / Personaldata.io (2025), *How Big Tech hides its outsourced African workforce*** — Examines secretive outsourcing practices by tech companies in Kenya.[^64]

### 6.5 News Media and Advertising Revenue

- **ITWeb South Africa (2025)** reports that Google does not compensate South African media for news content it displays. Referral traffic to media websites has declined as users consume AI-generated summaries, and Google's algorithmic structure tends to favor large foreign outlets over local or vernacular media.[^65]

### 6.6 Android and Mobile Security

- **ArXiv (2025), *Security Evaluation of Android apps in budget African smartphones*** — Examined 1,544 pre-installed APKs from seven low-cost Android devices (Infinix, Tecno, itel — Transsion brands that hold 51% of the African smartphone market). Found 145 applications (9%) disclosed sensitive data, 249 (16%) exposed critical components, and many presented additional risks including silent installation operations. Notes that while Android manufacturers are expected to follow Google's compliance guidelines, low-cost devices in African markets raise concerns about trade-offs between cost and security.[^66]

### 6.7 Data Privacy

- **BERA (2022), "Data privacy on the African continent: Opportunities, challenges and implications"** — A scoping review of privacy regulations across Africa, relevant to Google's data-intensive operations.[^67]
- **CIPESA, *Privacy Imperilled: Analysis of Surveillance, Encryption and Data Localisation Laws in Africa*** — Maps privacy-related laws across multiple African countries, documenting how biometric data collection, encryption limits, and broad surveillance powers erode privacy rights.[^68]
- **Scholarworks (2025), "Privacy and Data Protection in Africa: An Analysis of Adequacy"** — Evaluates data protection frameworks in South Africa, Ghana, Kenya, and Nigeria.[^69]

## 7. The Broader Context: Competing Narratives

The literature on Google's activities in Africa reveals a fundamental tension between two narratives:

**The development narrative**, advanced largely by Google-commissioned reports and institutional partners, emphasizes the company's role in closing the digital divide, creating economic opportunities, and building AI capabilities tailored to African needs. The Public First report's estimate of $16 billion in economic activity, the Equiano EIAs' projections of millions of jobs, and partnerships with the African Union and UN agencies all support this framing.[^8][^47][^3]

**The critical narrative**, found primarily in academic literature and NGO reports, frames Google's expansion as a new iteration of colonial resource extraction — with data, rather than minerals, as the resource. This literature questions who controls the infrastructure, who benefits from the data generated, and whether African countries are building genuine sovereignty or deepening dependency.[^52][^53][^55]

These narratives are not entirely contradictory. Equiano may genuinely reduce internet costs *and* extend Google's market dominance. AI research labs in Accra and Nairobi may produce locally relevant innovations *and* channel talent and data into Google's global ecosystem. The most sophisticated literature acknowledges this duality.

***

## 8. Gaps in the Literature

Several areas remain underexplored:

- **Long-term empirical assessments** of whether projected economic impacts from infrastructure investments (e.g., Equiano EIAs) materialized as modeled.
- **YouTube's creator economy** in Africa — while the Africa Creator Economy Report (2024) estimates the continent's creator market at $3 billion, there is limited scholarly analysis of Google/YouTube's specific role, revenue-sharing practices, and monetization policies in African markets.[^70]
- **Google Cloud adoption** by African governments and enterprises — mostly covered in press releases rather than independent analysis.
- **Environmental impacts** of data center and cable infrastructure construction across the continent.
- **Comparative analysis** of Google's approach versus Microsoft, Meta, Amazon, and Chinese tech companies (Huawei, Transsion) in Africa.
